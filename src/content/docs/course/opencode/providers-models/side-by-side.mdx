You hit this the moment you start trusting OpenCode with a real judgement call. `feedmill` dedupes items across dozens of feeds by hashing each one, and you've been quietly nervous about it: the same story syndicated through three feeds should collapse to one row in the reading queue, and once in a while it doesn't. So you ask. And because you've got two providers wired up from earlier in this chapter, you ask twice - once on a model from each - and they tell you opposite things.

```text
> is the dedupe hash in internal/dedupe collision-safe for our feed volume?

  [anthropic/claude-opus-4-5]
  It's fine. The hash keys on a normalized (title, link, published) tuple
  with FNV-64a; at a few hundred thousand items the collision probability
  is negligible. No change needed.
```

```text
> is the dedupe hash in internal/dedupe collision-safe for our feed volume?

  [openai/gpt-5.2]
  This is risky. FNV-64a is not collision-resistant, and you're keying on
  fields a feed controls - two different items with a crafted or coincident
  title/link pair can hash equal and one silently vanishes from the queue.
  Move to SHA-256 over the canonical item bytes.
```
{/* VERIFY (OPEN - version-dependent): `anthropic/claude-opus-4-5` and `openai/gpt-5.2` are valid `provider/model` slugs but not the newest as of June 2026 - opencode Zen already lists `opencode/claude-opus-4-8` and `opencode/gpt-5.5` (opencode.ai/docs/zen). They're fine as illustrative slugs, but model lineups churn; confirm the current direct-provider slugs before publishing (run `/models` in the TUI, or check the provider's docs). */}

One says ship it, the other says you have a latent data-loss bug. You can't tell who's right by re-reading the answers - they're both fluent and confident, and confidence is the one signal an LLM gives you for free regardless of whether it's correct. What you *can* do is the thing a single-vendor tool can't: stop treating this as a debate and turn it into an A/B across two providers.

## Why this is OpenCode's move

Claude Code can only ask Claude. Codex can only ask OpenAI. When either one hands you a confident answer, your only second opinion comes from the same vendor's lineup - there's no *other provider* in the room to disagree with it. OpenCode is provider-agnostic, and provider choice is **per-agent**: every agent carries its own `model` field, so two agents pointed at two different providers can run the identical prompt and you read the difference.

Be precise about what's the differentiator here, because it's narrower than "only OpenCode can run agents in parallel." It isn't parallel multi-agent work itself - OpenCode ships agent teams (a lead agent spawning teammate subagents, each with its own context), and Claude Code shipped that concept first. The thing a single-vendor tool genuinely can't do is put **two different providers** behind the same question. That's the lever: not that OpenCode can run two agents, but that the two agents can be backed by Anthropic and OpenAI at once. The disagreement above isn't a problem to resolve by gut feel; it's two data points you can lay side by side because the tool let you summon both - from two vendors.

That's the whole technique. You're not looking for the model that agrees with you. You're looking at *where two strong models diverge*, because the divergence is a map of exactly the claim that needs checking - here, "is FNV-64a collision-resistant, and does it matter at our scale and threat model."

## Set up two agents, one per provider

The clean way to make this repeatable is two thin subagents that differ only in their `model`. Drop two files in `.opencode/agents/` (the runtime reads the plural directory; the singular `.opencode/agent/` is only accepted for backwards compatibility):

```yaml
---
description: Second opinion, Anthropic side
mode: subagent
model: anthropic/claude-opus-4-5
permission:
  edit: deny
---
You are a reviewer. Answer the question directly and justify your
reasoning. Do not edit files.
```

```yaml
---
description: Second opinion, OpenAI side
mode: subagent
model: openai/gpt-5.2
permission:
  edit: deny
---
You are a reviewer. Answer the question directly and justify your
reasoning. Do not edit files.
```

Same prompt body, same read-only permission, different `model` - and because every provider is referenced in the one universal `provider/model` format, the only line that changes between the two files is the slug after `model:`. Call them `review-a` and `review-b`. Both are `edit: deny`, because a second opinion has no business touching the parsers; you want analysis, not action. (`mode: subagent`, the `model` field, and the nested `permission` block are all documented frontmatter - `permission` takes `ask`/`allow`/`deny` per tool.)

You invoke a subagent the way OpenCode invokes any subagent - by `@mention`. The transcript below shows both summoned from one prompt; if your build doesn't fan a dual mention out cleanly, run them as two turns (or the two headless `opencode run` invocations shown below) and you get the same A/B:

```text
> @review-a @review-b is the dedupe hash in internal/dedupe collision-safe
  for our feed volume? Read the code before answering, and be specific
  about the hash function and what we key on.

  ⏺ review-a (anthropic/claude-opus-4-5)
    read internal/dedupe/hash.go
    → FNV-64a over normalized (title, link, published). At your volume the
      birthday-bound collision risk is ~1e-9. Safe as a dedupe key. The
      real exposure isn't math - it's that the inputs are feed-controlled.

  ⏺ review-b (openai/gpt-5.2)
    read internal/dedupe/hash.go
    → FNV is a non-cryptographic hash; collisions are cheap to engineer
      and the keyed fields come straight from untrusted feeds. For a
      dedupe key fed by sources you don't control, use SHA-256.
```
{/* VERIFY (OPEN - undocumented behavior): @mention invocation of a subagent IS documented (opencode.ai/docs/agents), but mentioning TWO subagents in one prompt as a single *parallel* turn is NOT documented in core opencode - the docs only show single-mention examples. Prose is hedged to not assert parallel execution; the two-turn / two-`opencode run` fallback below is the guaranteed path. Recheck the dual-mention behavior against the pinned release before presenting it as parallel. */}

Read those two carefully and the "disagreement" has already half-collapsed. Both agents now agree on the *facts* - FNV-64a, keyed on feed-controlled fields. They diverge only on the *judgement*: is feed-controlled input a real threat for `feedmill` or a theoretical one? That's no longer a question about hashing. It's a question about your threat model, and now it's the only thing you have to decide. The A/B didn't pick a winner for you - it stripped away everything the models agreed on so the actual decision stands alone.

## Diff the outputs, don't just read them

Two short answers you can eyeball. But the comparison gets sharper the longer the outputs get, and eyeballing a pair of three-paragraph analyses is exactly where you miss the one sentence that matters. So make the comparison literal: run each side headless, capture its output, and diff the files. Running them as two separate `opencode run` invocations also sidesteps any question of whether two subagents truly run in parallel in one turn - each invocation is its own clean, non-interactive process.

```text
$ opencode run --agent review-a --format json \
    "is the dedupe hash in internal/dedupe collision-safe for our feed \
     volume? read the code first." > a.json

$ opencode run --agent review-b --format json \
    "is the dedupe hash in internal/dedupe collision-safe for our feed \
     volume? read the code first." > b.json

$ git diff --no-index a.json b.json
```

`opencode run` is the same non-interactive entry point you met earlier - `--agent` selects which agent answers, and because each agent pins its own provider, the two runs hit two different vendors with one identical prompt. There's no `--quiet`/`--print` flag to reach for; instead `run` takes `--format` with either `default` (formatted output) or `json` (raw JSON events), so for a clean, machine-diffable capture use `--format json` and diff that - or extract the final message from each and diff those. Either way the divergence is no longer something you're holding in your head - it's red and green on the screen, the agreed-on facts collapsing to unchanged lines and the genuine disagreement standing out as the only hunk that differs.

This is the part single-vendor tools can't give you - not because they lack a compare button, but because they have no *second provider* to compare against. The A/B only exists because OpenCode holds credentials for both providers at once and lets a per-agent `model` field send the same question down two paths to two vendors.

## When you don't want to pick the providers yourself

You don't have to know which two models to pit against each other. If you'd rather trust a vetted shortlist than reason about every provider's strengths, OpenCode's **Zen** is one way to get there - a curated, paid AI gateway that serves models the OpenCode team tested and tuned for the agent loop, behind a single key. Reference a Zen model the way you reference any other, as a `provider/model` slug where the provider is literally `opencode` - e.g. `opencode/gpt-5.5`. Pull your `review-a` / `review-b` pair from Zen and you've skipped the homework of figuring out which models are even worth comparing; you're charged per request through the gateway, which is the trade for not benchmarking the catalog yourself.

## What you actually decided

You started with a vendor argument you couldn't adjudicate and ended with a decision that was yours to make: both models agree the dedupe keys on feed-controlled fields, so whether to move from FNV-64a to SHA-256 comes down to whether a malicious or coincident feed dropping an item from your queue is a risk you'll accept. The two providers didn't settle it - they're not supposed to. They surfaced the exact claim worth checking and left the call where it belongs. That's the whole value of running two side by side: not a referee, but a sharper question.
