## What each chapter left you able to ask

Strip away the tool-specific mechanics and every chapter handed you one question you can now ask that you could not before:

1. [Getting started](/course/codex/getting-started/) - *what actually happened in that turn, and does the diff match what I asked for?*

3. [Approvals & sandbox](/course/codex/approvals-sandbox/) - *how far can this run reach on its own, and where does it have to stop and ask?*
4. [Rules](/course/codex/rules/) - *is this written down where Codex reads it - and is guidance enough, or does something need to enforce it?*
5. [Models & effort](/course/codex/models-effort/) - *does this task's shape actually earn the extra thinking, or is the pattern already decided?*

9. [Automation](/course/codex/automation/) - *what replaces me, stage by stage, when nobody's at the keyboard to catch the wrong turn?*

## The whole stack, priced by what it spends and holds

Every chapter priced its own move where a number answered a real question. Rather than replay those numbers in one grid, here is the same synthesis organized the way the course actually taught you to think - by what each move spends, what it holds, and whether it survives the session that built it:

- **Context.** The rules file (~115 estimated tokens a session, ~150 with the parse-path rule) informs every session from now on - and never enforces. The skill pays a ~90-token stub every session and loads ~300 more only when the situation fires. Sessions still start cold; what changed is what the cold session finds written down.
- **Capability.** Matching model and effort to task shape keeps pattern work cheap and gives design forks the depth they need - the profiles carry that judgment so it recurs. Delegation moved noisy reading out of the window that holds the answer.
- **Control.** The two dials, bundled into named profiles, bound what runs may touch. The hooks added the guarantees no instruction can give: the statements wall and the money-tests gate hold unattended, on the patterns they match. The sandbox is a wall; the rules are a wish; the hook is a rail.
- **Cost.** Nothing above was free. Rules and skills pay rent every session; MCP reach pays per loaded schema, standing; matched effort pays per turn. The unattended runs moved the cost of watching into the cost of wiring checks ahead of time - and the exit code is worth exactly what someone wired into it.

The through-line underneath all four: every fix here replaced a cost you paid every session with a cost you pay only when the world underneath the fix moves.

## The scenario, reconciled

The course defined its teaching scenario before you had installed anything: the same job - onboarding a fourth bank's CSV - costs 11 exchanges on a cold Monday and 2 by Friday, in the scenario's own bookkeeping. The reconciliation has two halves, and only one of them was ever about exchanges.

**Monday:** a cold session and a plain prompt - 11 exchanges of narrating the procedure, correcting misreadings, re-describing things the repo already knew. And the repo as Monday found it carried a second failure the count never measured: the amount parse in `app/importers/csv.py` ran money through a float, violating rules that were on disk and loaded the whole time.

That violation pre-dated the agent. It sat in a live money path with no pin and no gate, and nothing - written or otherwise - was going to catch it.

**Friday:** the 21-line `AGENTS.md` that has been on disk since before the course began - about 115 estimated tokens a session, 150 with the parse-path rule the rules chapter added - plus the `budget-build` profile drilled into a reflex take the same job to 6 exchanges: the exact checkpoint the skills chapter measured. The `import-bank-csv` skill takes it the rest of the way, from 6 to 2.

And the zero-violation half of Friday's promise is carried by machinery Monday never had: the `Decimal` parse, the pin that feeds `0.29` through and asserts `29`, and the hook that re-runs the money tests on every money edit.

11 minus 5 minus 4 lands on 2. That arithmetic is the scenario's own bookkeeping, not a set of isolated measurements: the 5 belongs to the rules-file-plus-profile combination, the 4 to the skill, and the effort dial spends tokens, not turns. [The honest edge of that arithmetic](/course/codex/daily-workflow/error-recovery/) is worth re-reading if the tidiness above sounds too clean.

## What this costs you now

Every fix in this course charged something, and this one charges later. A rules-file line is a snapshot of what was true the week it was written; the day a new rule enters the codebase and nobody updates the file, you are back to explaining it by hand, and you will not notice until the agent gets it wrong.

A profile is a snapshot of how much you trusted a piece of work when you named it. A skill is a snapshot of one procedure; the day a format shows up in a shape it never saw, you are the one who notices, extends, and re-teaches it.

The hooks are the same trade in a different currency: they hold on the exact strings they can see, and everything else walks past them. None of that is a defect in the plan. It is the plan's own shape, restated: every fix here replaced a cost you paid every session with a cost you pay only when the world underneath the fix moves.

**Maintaining the files the course added - one rule, three profiles, one skill, two hook scripts, one workflow - is now part of running `budgetcli` (the course's running example, a small self-hosted personal-finance API in Python and FastAPI), the same way the code itself is.**

## The whole job

Strip away the tool and every chapter taught one thing. AI coding agents are extraordinary at the *general* - broad, fast, fluent across languages - and blind to the *particular*: they do not know your codebase, your conventions, your domain, or that on `budgetcli` money is integer cents and never a float. You are the inverse - slower, narrower, but you know all of that cold.

And the course added the second half of that lesson: knowing the rule is not what keeps it true. The rules file carried the money rule from before day one, and the float parse shipped anyway. What keeps a rule true is machinery - a hardened parse, a pin, a gate - and knowing *which one a situation needs* is now part of what you know.

That is the discipline. Not prompting tricks, not a clever command - *closing the gap* between what you know and what the agent knows, on purpose, every time, with the right container: rules that inform, profiles that bound, skills that package, hooks that enforce. The work on `budgetcli` is done. Now go close it on the repo that is actually yours.
