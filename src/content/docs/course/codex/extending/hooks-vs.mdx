Recall the two hooks from the wiring lesson (or take them as given): a `PreToolUse` hook that inspects every shell command for a path into the raw bank statements and blocks the rewrite, and a `PostToolUse` hook that re-runs the money tests after every edit-tool edit under `app/importers/` and `app/services/`. In the example run the first blocked two routes to the same forbidden rewrite, and let a third pass untouched - a script whose command line named no statement path, invisible to a check that reads the command string. The second caught a float walking back into the money parse inside the turn that wrote it. Neither is a loose end - they are the facts this lesson exists to explain. You have now governed Codex three different ways, and knowing which one a constraint actually needs is what turns "I set up a guardrail" into a guardrail that's still there at 2am.

Lay the three side by side:

- A **rule** (a line in [`AGENTS.md`](/course/codex/rules/)) is *context*. It tells the model what's true and what you want, and the model weighs it when it decides. It lives in the context window - the real 21-line file costs about 115 estimated tokens every session, the [rules chapter's arithmetic](/course/codex/rules/first-agents-md/) - which means it can be compacted out, buried under a long session, or simply under-weighted on a given turn. You're trusting the model to remember and to care.
- The **approvals and sandbox dials** (the course's trust controls, covered in [their chapter](/course/codex/approvals-sandbox/)) are *environmental enforcement*. They're stronger than a rule - [the sandbox is a genuine wall](/course/codex/approvals-sandbox/), and the agent can't write outside its writable roots or hit the network when you've turned it off. But they govern *capability*: which tools the agent may invoke, where it may write, whether it may reach the network. They answer "may the agent do this *kind* of thing?" - not "is the content of this *specific* command acceptable?"
- A **hook** is *deterministic, model-independent code* at a supported lifecycle point. Its handler runs outside the model's reasoning and costs zero standing prefix - nothing about a hook rides the window just by existing. But a hook is not silent by nature: when one fires, its output can reach the model, as deny feedback, added context, or a continuation prompt, capped at roughly 2,500 tokens by default. The model's reasoning, its mood, its remaining budget - none of that is inside the hook.

The sharpest way to feel the difference: a rule routes through the model's judgment, the sandbox through a capability check. **A hook routes through neither.** That's why the statements guarantee had to be a hook. "Never rewrite a raw statement" isn't a fact to remember, and it isn't a capability to revoke - reading statements is the legitimate work the widened `budget-build` writable roots were opened for. It's a condition on the *content* of one command, checked at the instant it is attempted. Only a hook expresses that.

A number worth sitting with. The course's 21-line `AGENTS.md` costs about 115 estimated tokens, every session, forever. The two hooks cost zero standing prefix - the scripts never enter the window by existing. When one fires, its denial or feedback adds tokens to that turn, and only that turn. A rule buys judgment, and you pay for the window whether or not the model uses it well. A hook buys a guarantee for free, and pays only when it speaks.

## A decision rule

So which do you reach for? The test is about what kind of guarantee the situation demands:

- **State a preference or a fact** the agent should weigh - "money is integer cents, never float," "the keyword table is fragile, be careful." That's a [rule](/course/codex/rules/). You want the model *informed*, and you accept that it's the model deciding.
- **Constrain what the agent can do or where it can reach** - read-only on this run, no network, nothing outside the workspace and `~/budget-data`. That's the [approvals and sandbox model](/course/codex/approvals-sandbox/). You want a wall around a *capability or a path*, with no per-command judgment involved.
- **Check a condition at a supported lifecycle point** - no rewrite of a raw statement, the money tests after every money edit, a risky command flagged before it runs. That's a **hook**. You want a deterministic guardrail that does not depend on the agent remembering anything; pair it with sandbox, file permissions, and CI when the condition is security-critical.

The fourth container from the last chapter slots in under these three: a **skill** packages a procedure. It doesn't inform, bound, or enforce - it *acts*, on recognition. Four mechanisms, four verbs: rules inform, skills package, hooks enforce, sandbox bounds. The rule still needs a clear approval setting (the trust dials) around it; the hook needs no remembering but is no replacement for infrastructure-level enforcement. Hooks also run the positive version - firing a test suite after a matching action, not just blocking - the money-tests gate is exactly that shape.

## Why this is the hinge of the whole chapter

That last property - *a deterministic check at a supported hook point* - is why hooks matter the moment you stop watching. Sitting at the TUI you are still part of the approval boundary; a forgotten rule you'd catch in the diff. Its value shows up when you're *not* there - the report regenerating on a schedule, the import check running in CI, a turn finishing at 2am. In those runs, combine trusted hooks with sandboxing, least-privilege credentials, and CI gates - never one hook as the only wall.

Put the statements hook on the ladder above and it's the one that holds identically in all three situations - watching, three hours and a compaction later, unattended at 2am - because the check runs on the rail, not in the window, and no compaction can reach it. The `AGENTS.md` money rule sitting next to it doesn't get that guarantee, and never will, no matter how clearly it's worded.

Which is the whole reason this chapter came before the next one. `budgetcli` (the course's running-example repo) now holds both halves of what unattended work needs: **reach** - a rate-lookup tool wired over MCP (the Model Context Protocol, the external-tool connection standard from [the reach lesson](/course/codex/extending/mcp-connect/); a constructed feature, real wiring) - and **gates** - the statements wall and the money-tests check, which no argument can sway.

## Reach and gates, priced against each other

Both moves from this chapter changed what Codex can do, and they price almost nothing alike. The rate server costs roughly 128 estimated tokens for one loaded tool's schema - standing from registration where schemas inject up front, from first use where Codex defers them - and every later turn keeps paying once the schema has loaded, whether the tool is called again or not. The hooks cost zero standing prefix (the scripts never ride the window); denial feedback adds tokens only to the turn it fires in. The server is read-only reach, its real risk being what you connect next and stop paying attention to; the gates shrink blast radius to nothing on the patterns they match, and anything the command strings do not literally contain still gets through.

That comparison is the chapter's whole argument. Reach costs you window space, forever, whether you use it. A gate costs nothing while it stands silent, and pays only when it speaks - but only on the exact thing its strings can see. Neither one is free in the way that matters: one spends window, the other spends your foresight in writing the matcher correctly the first time.

The course's villain from one last new angle: each session still starts cold except what is written down where Codex reads by default - and what is written down informs, never enforces. What's in front of Codex now includes a rate door that wasn't there and two walls it can't argue with, both put there on purpose, both priced.

One thing in this whole stack has never happened yet: none of it has run without a human at the keyboard. What runs when nobody watches - and what fail-shut even means when there's no one to fail in front of - is [the automation chapter](/course/codex/automation/).
